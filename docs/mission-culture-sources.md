# Mission: culture and language source notes

Checked 14 November 2026. Prepared content is in `work/mission-cultures.json` in the task workspace, outside the website checkout. Its eight region IDs preserve the existing atlas order. Each item contains a headline, a 30-word summary, a 10-word value, an 80-word optional context, a 20-word care connection, sources and a scope note. Word counts use whitespace-separated words.

## Editorial approach

The entries describe independent cultural work. They do not claim Neorons presence, delivery, consultation or partnership in these places. They do not imply that learning a language prevents suicide, or assign a health condition to a cultural identity. `careConnection` and `culturalValue` are editorial principles for respectful design, informed by the sources; they are not quotations, clinical findings or claims that communities have endorsed Neorons.

The sources below are cultural organisations, Indigenous representative institutions or public language institutions describing their own work. The small number of Indigenous words retained are documented language or organisation names, not invented slogans. No sacred patterns, ceremonial symbols, voices or cultural artwork are proposed for reuse.

## Inuit communities in Nunavut

[Nunavut Tunngavik Incorporated’s Uqausirngaq Fund guidance](https://www.tunngavik.com/files/2025/05/Uqausirngaq-Fund-Application-ENG.pdf) describes education, skills, community programming, documentation, media and arts. [NTI’s October 2026 announcement](https://tunngavik.com/news/nti-board-approves-key-investments-language-health-and-community-wellness) confirms continuing Inuit-led language priorities.

Scope: Nunavut Inuit, including eligible initiatives outside Nunavut. The entry does not describe all Inuit regions or claim uniform language use. Programme examples are documented activities, not Neorons achievements. No funding figures are reused.

## Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples

[First Languages Australia’s language overview](https://www.firstlanguages.org.au/languages) explains connections between language, people, land and knowledge. Its [engagement guidance](https://www.firstlanguages.org.au/engaging-with-language-groups) asks organisations to work with local communities, avoid assumptions about language backgrounds, respect oral traditions and allow relationships time to develop.

Scope: distinct nations and language communities. Traditional languages, Aboriginal English and creoles are not interchangeable. “Country” follows the source’s cultural usage; no singular pan-Australian protocol is asserted.

## Māori communities in Aotearoa

[Te Mātāwai’s account of its purpose](https://tematawai.maori.nz/purpose/what-we-do?page=1) places solutions with whānau, communities and iwi. Its [2024–2027 Statement of Intent](https://www.tematawai.maori.nz/assets/Corporate-Documents/WEB-Te-Matawai-SOI-20242027-English-v11.pdf) describes community leadership and everyday language use at home.

Scope: a Māori-led revitalisation approach, not a description of every Māori household. The care connection explicitly leaves family involvement to the individual’s wishes. Māori terminology is used only where verified in these sources; no translated motto or invented quotation is supplied.

## Sámi communities

The Sámi Parliament of Finland explains [language nests](https://samediggi.fi/en/areas-of-expertise/sami-languages/language-nest/) and documents [the local history and administration of cultural and language-nest activities](https://samediggi.fi/en/sami-language-nests-and-cultural-activities-in-finland/).

Scope: the institutional examples are in Finland and concern distinct Sámi languages. They should not be presented as the administrative arrangements of every country in Sápmi. The copy does not suggest that Sámi is one language or that every family participates in a language nest.

## Alaska Native communities

The Alaska Native Heritage Center’s [own account](https://www.alaskanative.net/about/) identifies its living-cultural mission, Indigenous origins, statewide scope and location on the traditional territory of the Native Village of Eklutna. Its [cultural programming page](https://www.alaskanative.net/learn/cultural-programming/) documents educational and creative activities.

Scope: a statewide cultural resource in Anchorage. This is not a depiction of all Alaska Native peoples as a single culture, a claim about Qungasvik, or an assertion about specific health outcomes.

## First Nations in Canada

The [First Peoples’ Cultural Council’s overview](https://fpcc.ca/about-us/overview/) identifies First Nations leadership, community-defined priorities, cultural protocols and its language, arts and heritage work.

Scope: FPCC’s mandate is British Columbia. The Canada heading is geographic; the actual example remains explicitly regional. First Nations, Inuit and Métis are not conflated. The final sentence about control over sharing knowledge is an editorial implication of community direction and protocol, not a quotation from FPCC.

## Guarani communities in Brazil

[Comissão Guarani Yvyrupa’s account of its organisation](https://www.yvyrupa.org.br/sobre-a-cgy/) describes southern and southeastern Brazil, territorial rights, assemblies and the role of leaders and elders. Its [June 2022 education report](https://www.yvyrupa.org.br/2022/06/14/povos-indigenas-querem-implementacao-de-diretrizes-curriculares-para-a-educacao-escolar-indigena-em-sao-paulo-sp/) documents teachers and leaders developing school curriculum proposals.

Scope: the education effort included Guarani and other Indigenous peoples in São Paulo. The copy says proposals, not enacted law. It does not attribute Guarani organising to Neorons or turn one regional example into a universal account of Guarani life. Portuguese material is paraphrased in English without quoted translations.

## Maya communities in Guatemala

The Academia de Lenguas Mayas de Guatemala’s [institutional plan](https://almg.org.gt/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Plan-Operativo-Anual-3.pdf), section 6.2–6.3, sets out its mission and cultural-identity principles. The [Guatemalan government’s institutional directory](https://directorio.gdav.gob.gt/item/academia-de-lenguas-mayas-alm/) confirms language research, teaching, dictionaries, translation and cultural work.

Scope: distinct Maya languages and communities in Guatemala. No single “Maya language” is implied; Xinka and Garífuna are not classified as Maya. The PDF’s mission and identity passages were available in the indexed primary text; a direct browser fetch timed out. The public-directory page was opened successfully and independently confirms the core language-work claims.
